# Literature Review of Amendments to the Current QR Finder Pipeline

## Main diagnosis

Your current pipeline, as described in the attached note, has two distinct failure modes: a local **\(\pi/2\)** orientation ambiguity at the single-finder level, and an **independent 8-DOF per-finder homography** that improves local fit but destroys the inter-finder consistency needed for association. In your own description, the rho-based corners still associate correctly, while the homography-refined corners do not. That is a very strong clue that the local projective refinement is solving the wrong problem at the wrong stage. fileciteturn0file0

The literature-backed pattern in mainstream QR implementations is different. ZXing finds finder-pattern **centers**, orders the three finders globally, estimates module size from inter-finder black-white-black runs, and then builds **one global perspective transform** for the QR code. It does not independently refine each finder with its own projective warp before association. citeturn5view2turn5view1turn23view0turn3view0 Quirc is the closest thing to a “local + global” design: it computes a local 7×7 perspective transform for each capstone to get that capstone’s center and corner order, but then explicitly rotates the capstones into a common grid frame, measures grid size, estimates the alignment pattern, and sets up **one global QR-grid perspective transform**, which it then improves by maximizing a global fitness over capstones, timing pattern, and alignment patterns. citeturn17view0turn17view3turn25view0turn17view2turn25view3 OpenCV’s QR detector also follows the same high-level pattern: localize three markers, compute a quadrilateral for the whole code, and then proceed with a global transform for rectification. citeturn18view0turn5view5turn18view1turn18view2turn18view3

So the central recommendation from the literature and from production implementations is this: **do not let local per-finder projective refinement define the geometry used for global association**. Use local fitting to obtain measurements; use a **single global QR geometry** to enforce consistency. citeturn3view0turn17view2turn18view1

## What standard implementations actually do

ZXing’s QR pipeline is a clean reference implementation of the “local detections, then global model” idea. `FinderPatternFinder` first searches for 1:1:3:1:1 candidates and cross-checks them horizontally and vertically. It then chooses the best three candidates based on **similar module size** and whether they form something close to an **isosceles right triangle**. After that, `ResultPoint.orderBestPatterns()` orders the three points using pairwise distances and a cross product to get a consistent orientation. Only then does `Detector.processFinderPatternInfo()` estimate module size, dimension, and construct one perspective transform for the whole code. citeturn5view2turn5view1turn23view0turn3view0

Quirc is even more relevant to your design because it does perform a form of local finder refinement. When a “capstone” is found, it computes the ring corners for that finder and sets up a **local 7×7 perspective transform** for the capstone, then maps the canonical point \((3.5,3.5)\) to obtain the capstone center. But crucially, quirc does **not** treat those local projective fits as independent final truth. It constructs the global QR hypothesis from three capstones, rotates each capstone so that corner 0 is consistently “top-left with respect to the grid,” estimates grid size from timing-pattern structure, optionally locates an alignment pattern, sets up one grid-reading perspective transform, and then “jiggles” that global transform to improve a score that explicitly checks capstones, timing pattern, and alignment patterns together. citeturn17view0turn17view3turn25view0turn25view2turn17view2turn25view3

OpenCV’s detector again lands on the same side of the design question. It localizes three points from horizontal and vertical line structure, clusters them, orders them with `fixationPoints`, flood-fills outward from those local points, builds a hull, and extracts the transformation quadrilateral for the whole code. The implementation is built around a **global quadrilateral / global transform**, not around three unrelated per-finder homographies. citeturn22view2turn22view0turn18view0turn18view1turn18view2turn18view3

That means your present architecture is misaligned with the dominant practice in two ways. First, it is trying to resolve a fundamentally global orientation issue at the single-finder level. Second, it is allowing a physically over-flexible per-finder model to overwrite the geometry that should remain globally shared by one planar QR symbol. citeturn23view0turn17view3turn17view2

## Why the current two breakages are expected

The first breakage, the **\(\pi/2\) ambiguity**, is not surprising. A single QR finder pattern is close to a square with fourfold rotational symmetry, so any local orientation estimate based only on the finder itself is ambiguous up to 90 degrees unless you inject information from the surrounding QR structure. Your own method explicitly encodes this by using a 4-fold moment. fileciteturn0file0 Production readers mostly avoid this problem by not trying to assign a globally meaningful quadrant to a single finder in isolation. ZXing resolves orientation only after selecting a valid triplet and ordering the three finder centers globally. Quirc resolves capstone corner numbering only after constructing a reference line from the candidate QR geometry and then rotating each capstone into a common frame. citeturn23view0turn17view1turn17view3

The second breakage, **per-finder homography destroying colinearity**, is exactly what one should expect from giving each finder an independent 8-DOF projective warp. A planar QR code has one actual image homography, not three unrelated local projective maps. If each finder is allowed to move its corners independently to satisfy a local edge objective, then pairwise segment colinearity across finders will generally degrade even in clean frontoparallel cases. Your observation that this happens even on trivially clean data strongly suggests model mismatch, not merely parameter tuning trouble. fileciteturn0file0 The fact that mainstream implementations do not use independent final per-finder homographies for association is consistent with that diagnosis. citeturn3view0turn17view2turn18view1

There is also a statistical modeling issue in your angle-estimation stage. Your doubled-angle formulation is trying to represent **axial** data: a line normal and its antipode are equivalent, and after doubling, orthogonal families become antipodal structure on a circle. In directional statistics, antipodally symmetric orientation data are more naturally modeled with an **axial distribution** such as the Bingham family, which is specifically designed for 180-degree symmetry. citeturn24academia0turn24search6 EM on symmetric mixture models is also well known to be sensitive to initialization and local optima. So if the two-von-Mises EM is unstable on exactly symmetric data, that is partly a modeling issue and partly a sign to check the implementation. citeturn14academia3turn24academia0

## Amendments that are best supported by the literature

The strongest amendment is architectural: **split local measurement from global geometry**. Keep the per-finder stage, but make it output only robust local measurements such as center, module pitch, two edge-family normals, and perhaps outer-edge offsets. Do **not** let that stage output final corners that overwrite global association geometry. Instead, use those local measurements to build and score global QR hypotheses. This adjustment is directly aligned with ZXing, quirc, and OpenCV. citeturn3view0turn17view2turn18view1

The second amendment is to move orientation disambiguation from the single-finder stage to the **triplet or global stage**. The practical analogue in quirc is `rotate_capstone`: first form a QR hypothesis from three capstones, then rotate each local corner order to be consistent with the global grid direction. The practical analogue in ZXing is `orderBestPatterns()`: first identify a valid triplet, then orient it using distances and cross product. In other words, keep your 4-fold histogram or axial estimator as a way to estimate the **unordered pair of orthogonal axes**, but postpone the decision of which one is “u” and which one is “v,” and which side is inward versus outward, until you have a global hypothesis. citeturn23view0turn17view3

The third amendment is to replace pairwise corner-colinearity association with **triplet-level geometric validation**. The most standard ingredients are (a) center geometry close to an isosceles right triangle, (b) module-size compatibility across the three finders, and then (c) a global perspective or grid score using timing/alignment/capstone structure. ZXing already uses similar-module-size plus right-triangle logic before perspective rectification. Quirc goes further by using grid-size checks and a global fitness over timing, capstones, and alignment patterns. That is much more robust to local corner deformation than checking whether two independently warped outer segments are colinear. citeturn5view1turn23view0turn25view0turn25view1turn25view3

The fourth amendment is to simplify the local deformation model. If you do want a local refined geometry per finder, the code evidence suggests that a small local model is acceptable, but it should be used as a **measurement model**, not a final independent state. Quirc treats each finder as a local capstone with corners and a local perspective map, but the final grid perspective is still global. citeturn17view0turn17view2 For your pipeline, the safest practical substitute for the current 8-DOF per-finder homography would be one of two options. Either use only the rho-based line intersections for association and reserve all projective refinement for the global stage, or restrict local refinement to a line-family / affine / Jacobian-type model that cannot freely destroy inter-finder consistency. This recommendation is an inference from how production QR detectors are structured, but it is a strong one. citeturn3view0turn17view2turn18view1

The fifth amendment concerns the von-Mises EM step. If the only purpose of that step is to recover the two edge families, then a full 2-component circular mixture is probably the wrong tool. Your data are naturally axial and symmetric; a 4-fold moment or an axial distribution is a better fit to that symmetry class. The Bingham family is explicitly designed for antipodally symmetric directions, which is much closer to the geometry of line normals than an unconstrained two-component circular mixture. citeturn24academia0turn24search6 In practice, that means either removing the EM stage entirely, or reducing it to a deterministic or axial estimator and using the triplet/global stage to resolve the residual quadrant ambiguity. citeturn24academia0turn14academia3

## Answers to the specific questions

On whether existing QR libraries use per-finder local refinement: the source-backed answer is **mostly no for the final geometry, with one important nuance**. ZXing does not do independent per-finder geometric refinement in the way your current pipeline does; it uses finder centers, global ordering, module-size estimates, and one global perspective transform. citeturn5view2turn23view0turn3view0 Quirc does compute local capstone corners and a local 7×7 transform per finder, but then it explicitly forces those capstones into a common global frame and estimates one global grid transform, which it refines against global QR structure. citeturn17view0turn17view3turn17view2turn25view3 So the literature-backed amendment for your code is: if you keep per-finder refinement, do **not** let it define the final corners used for association; use it only to provide better local measurements to a global QR fit. citeturn17view2turn3view0

On how standard implementations resolve the \(\pi/2\) ambiguity: they generally do **not** try to solve it from a single finder alone. ZXing orders the three finder points globally with pairwise distances and a cross product. Quirc rotates each capstone relative to the grid’s reference line so that corner 0 becomes globally consistent. OpenCV similarly moves from localized markers to a globally ordered quadrilateral. citeturn23view0turn17view3turn18view1 The practical implication is that the 1:1:3:1:1 profile itself is not enough to pick one of the four square quadrants. You need a global cue such as finder-triplet arrangement, timing-pattern direction, or quiet-zone asymmetry in context. citeturn23view0turn25view1turn18view0

On why the two-mode von-Mises EM fails on a clean square: a total failure on a noiseless synthetic square probably does indicate **some implementation or fallback bug**, because the geometry itself is not pathological in the sense of being impossible to represent. But there is also a real modeling issue: once you double the angles, you have axial data with antipodal symmetry, and the natural statistical object is not an unconstrained 2-component von-Mises mixture but an axial distribution. Symmetric mixtures plus equal weights make EM particularly fragile because the likelihood landscape is flat under label swaps and often shallow around symmetric configurations. citeturn24academia0turn14academia3 So the most accurate answer is: **probably both**. There may be a bug, but even a bug-free EM is not the cleanest formulation here. citeturn24academia0turn14academia3

On association methods more robust than pairwise colinearity: yes, and the best-supported ones are **triplet and grid consistency methods**. ZXing uses module-size consistency and right-triangle geometry at the finder-center level. Quirc uses center geometry, capstone corner ordering, timing-pattern-based grid-size checks, alignment-pattern search, and a global grid fitness. OpenCV builds a whole-code quadrilateral from three localization points and their regional support. These strategies are all more robust to local corner deformation than testing whether one edge from finder A is colinear with one edge from finder B. citeturn5view1turn23view0turn25view0turn25view1turn25view3turn18view1

On whether it is standard to decompose detection into local per-finder estimation followed by global perspective, or to estimate a single global homography directly: the most accurate summary is that **production pipelines do both, but with a strict division of labor**. Local stages are used to detect and characterize finder patterns; the final geometry is usually enforced with **one global QR model**. ZXing is almost entirely in that camp. Quirc is the same, but with richer local capstone geometry before global perspective setup and optimization. OpenCV again follows the same pattern. What is **not** standard is letting each finder carry an independent final projective warp into the association stage. citeturn3view0turn17view0turn17view2turn18view1

## Recommended update to the plan

The most defensible amendment is to change the role of your per-finder homography. Keep steps 1 through 4 of your local fitting as measurement extraction; replace step 5’s “homography corners become the output” with “homography, if retained, is only a local auxiliary fit.” For association and triplet formation, use the **rho-based corners or even finder centers and local scales**, not the independently warped homography corners. Then estimate a **single global homography** from the selected triplet and optionally refine it jointly against all available outer-edge pixels, timing-pattern samples, and alignment-pattern evidence. This is the design most strongly supported by ZXing, quirc, and OpenCV. citeturn3view0turn17view2turn25view3turn18view1

A good concrete replacement for the current association stage would be: first, score candidate triplets using finder centers, similar module size, and right-triangle geometry. Second, once a triplet exists, assign consistent per-finder corner order using a global reference line, in the style of `orderBestPatterns()` or `rotate_capstone()`. Third, fit one QR homography and score it using reprojection error plus timing/alignment support. Only after that should you consider any local geometric refinement, and even then it should be constrained to improve the single shared QR warp rather than replace it. citeturn23view0turn17view3turn25view1turn25view3

For the local orientation stage, the cleanest amendment is to stop asking it to solve a problem it cannot solve alone. Use the 4-fold estimator, or a simpler axial estimator, only to recover an **unordered orthogonal frame** for the finder. Drop the 2-mode von-Mises EM unless you can show it consistently improves synthetic data. If you keep a probabilistic treatment, switch to an axial model rather than a two-component circular mixture. Then let the triplet/global stage resolve quadrant assignment. citeturn24academia0turn14academia3

If you want one sentence that captures the literature-backed fix, it is this: **treat per-finder fitting as a measurement problem and treat QR geometry as a single global projective object**. That is the design choice most clearly supported by the implementations and statistical modeling literature I could verify. citeturn3view0turn17view2turn24academia0
